## 1. Introduction

Respiratory health outcomes such as asthma exhibit pronounced geographic patterning, reflecting the combined influence of environmental exposures, socioeconomic conditions, and access to resources that vary across space. In spatial epidemiology, these patterned disparities are not treated as statistical noise but as consequential signals that can reveal how place-based risks and vulnerabilities structure population health. Small-area health analyses are therefore central both to scientific inference and to policy: they support the identification of localized clusters, the targeting of interventions, and the evaluation of environmental justice concerns in ways that coarser administrative summaries can obscure. Within this tradition, spatial epidemiology has emphasized the need to integrate health outcomes with geographically referenced environmental and social determinants to understand how disease burdens align with the built and natural environment (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1].

A substantial body of research has developed GIScience and quantitative geographic methods for relating environmental hazards and neighborhood characteristics to health-relevant outcomes. Early and continuing tract-based approaches demonstrate the value of census tracts as a pragmatic compromise between data availability, interpretability, and the ability to detect meaningful within-county heterogeneity, including for environmentally mediated exposures (Sargent et al., 1997) [REF_10]. More recent work has advanced spatial analytic toolkits for health mapping and neighborhood inference, including cluster detection and neighborhood-level modeling approaches that leverage administrative health data and area-based covariates (Kolak et al., 2019) [REF_11], as well as methods for addressing incomplete geocoding or uncertain geographic identifiers when estimating neighborhood associations for asthma-related outcomes (Bozigar et al., 2020) [REF_12]. Parallel environmental justice research has used spatial statistical models to evaluate how hazardous land uses and pollution sources correlate with socioeconomic and demographic disadvantage and how such relationships may be intersectional (Cannon, 2020) [REF_6]; (Cannon, 2021) [REF_7]. Complementary GIS-based exposure modeling—including proximity, density, and clustering approaches—has been applied to characterize exposure to airborne pollutants and facility-related risks in urban settings (Shock, 2021) [REF_8] and in large metropolitan regions using spatial exposure surfaces (Sun, 2013) [REF_9]. In North Carolina specifically, geographically explicit public health research has demonstrated how environmental exposures and access-related geographic factors can be analyzed jointly in population health questions (Kalkbrenner, 2019) [REF_4] and how disease ecology perspectives can be operationalized using spatially referenced socioeconomic and environmental determinants (Root, 2019) [REF_5]. Collectively, this literature establishes (i) the relevance of small-area spatial health analysis for environmental exposure questions and (ii) a practical set of GIScience methods—proximity metrics, tract-level modeling, and stratified or interaction-based assessments—to interrogate heterogeneity in exposure–outcome relationships.

Despite these advances, important gaps remain in how GIS-based health surveillance and environmental justice research are operationalized for fine-scale, decision-relevant inference. Although national and regional discussions of GIS-driven health information infrastructures emphasize the promise of spatially enabled surveillance for evidence-based policy, they also highlight persistent challenges in translating GIS capacity into routine, fine-grained analytic assessments that can inform targeted interventions—particularly when surveillance systems are not designed to support small-area environmental exposure linkage at scale (Boulos, 2004) [REF_2]. In addition, although research on spatial patterning in North Carolina has demonstrated that demographic and institutional conditions vary systematically across space and are consequential for observed outcomes, many studies focus on outcomes outside environmental health (e.g., labor and education-related mobility), leaving the implications for tract-level environmental exposure–health relationships less directly assessed (Sun, 2018) [REF_3]. More broadly, these limitations matter because environmental hazards and social vulnerability are both spatially clustered; without tract-level, spatially explicit modeling frameworks that explicitly evaluate effect modification by local socioeconomic and demographic context, analyses risk masking heterogeneity that is central to environmental justice interpretation and place-based policy targeting (Boulos, 2004) [REF_2]; (Sun, 2018) [REF_3]. This study responds to that need by focusing on a tract-scale assessment in North Carolina that explicitly tests whether the association between hazardous waste facility proximity and asthma prevalence varies with socioeconomic and demographic context.

Accordingly, this research asks: How does proximity to hazardous waste facilities influence spatial patterns of asthma prevalence across census tracts of North Carolina, and how do these relationships vary spatially by socioeconomic and demographic context? To address this question, the study pursues the following objective: **(1)** quantify the association between tract-level asthma prevalence and proximity to hazardous waste facilities across North Carolina census tracts while evaluating whether this association differs by tract socioeconomic and demographic conditions. Empirically, the study integrates four data components aligned to the census-tract scale: North Carolina tract geometries, georeferenced hazardous waste facility locations, tract-level asthma prevalence, and tract socioeconomic/demographic attributes from the American Community Survey. Analytically, it combines global regression modeling with interaction terms and complementary subgroup (stratified) modeling to evaluate effect modification in ways that are interpretable for environmental health screening and environmental justice assessment, consistent with established tract-based small-area health analytics (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]; (Sargent et al., 1997) [REF_10]; (Kolak et al., 2019) [REF_11]. While the primary emphasis is explanatory rather than causal, the study is designed to provide tract-scale evidence on whether proximity–asthma relationships appear uniform or context-dependent, thereby motivating future work on local modeling and targeted public health practice.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the study area, data sources, and construction of tract-level measures for asthma prevalence, hazardous waste facility proximity, and socioeconomic and demographic covariates. Section 3 presents the modeling framework, including global ordinary least squares specifications with robust inference, interaction-based tests for contextual modification, and stratified models for subgroup comparison. Section 4 reports the empirical results and summarizes how associations differ across model specifications and contextual groupings. Section 5 discusses implications for environmental justice screening and spatial health surveillance in North Carolina, outlines limitations, and identifies directions for subsequent spatially local and policy-oriented research.
