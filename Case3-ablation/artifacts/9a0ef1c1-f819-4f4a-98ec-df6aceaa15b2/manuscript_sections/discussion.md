## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal Findings
Across North Carolina census tracts, asthma prevalence exhibited pronounced spatial clustering, and hazardous-waste proximity was associated with tract-level asthma prevalence even after adjustment for multiple socioeconomic and demographic covariates. At the same time, the analyses showed that (i) tract context variables (poverty, Black population share, and income) accounted for substantial variation in asthma prevalence in the global model, and (ii) evidence for contextual modification of the proximity–asthma relationship depended strongly on how heterogeneity was operationalized: interaction terms added comparatively little explanatory power, whereas stratified models and threshold-based comparisons suggested sharper differences across poverty and racialized composition strata. Residual spatial autocorrelation remained after global modeling, indicating that important spatial processes were not fully captured by the included proximity and compositional measures. The remainder of the discussion interprets (1) the meaning of a detectable but context-embedded proximity association, (2) why interaction tests and stratified comparisons can disagree, and (3) what persistent spatial structure implies for tract-based environmental health screening.

### 4.2 Interpretation

#### 4.2.1 Proximity as an exposure proxy within a broader place-based risk structure
**Pattern.** The tract-level proximity metric (log distance from tract centroids to the nearest hazardous-waste facility) co-varied with asthma prevalence in the expected direction for an environmental hazard proxy—higher prevalence closer to sites—yet the bivariate relationship was dispersed and the multivariable results indicated that social context measures were at least as salient as proximity in the global model. This is consistent with the map-based evidence of spatially contiguous tracts with high or low asthma prevalence and with the strong covariation of asthma with multiple indicators of socioeconomic position and racialized composition.

**Mechanism (with boundary conditions).** One plausible mechanism is that hazardous-waste facility proximity serves as a coarse indicator of a bundle of environmental and infrastructural exposures—industrial co-location, trucking corridors, cumulative air toxics, and land-use histories—that can plausibly aggravate asthma burden in nearby populations. Environmental justice scholarship has long emphasized that hazardous land uses and environmental harms often correlate with patterns of disadvantage and political marginalization, producing spatially clustered exposure profiles rather than isolated risks (Cannon, 2020) [REF_6]; (Cannon, 2021) [REF_7]. Under this view, the proximity coefficient should not be interpreted as the association with a single pollutant pathway; it is more consistent with a “place-based exposure package” operating at neighborhood scales (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. Importantly, this mechanism has clear boundary conditions: a nearest-facility distance metric will be less informative where (i) emissions or health-relevant exposures do not decay monotonically with distance, (ii) wind, hydrology, or topography reorient exposure away from simple Euclidean buffers, (iii) the facility registry includes heterogeneous site types with very different emission profiles, or (iv) the relevant exposure is dominated by non-facility sources (e.g., household allergens, wildfire smoke, or traffic) that are spatially patterned for reasons unrelated to hazardous-waste siting. The substantial dispersion in the bivariate plot is consistent with these boundary conditions: tracts at similar distances can nonetheless face very different exposure mixtures and vulnerability profiles.

**Engagement with prior literature.** The finding that proximity-based exposure indicators can reveal tract-level gradients, but with substantial within-distance heterogeneity, is consistent with prior GIS-based environmental exposure work that uses proximity or spatial exposure surfaces as screening tools rather than definitive causal measures (Sun, 2013) [REF_9]; (Shock, 2021) [REF_8]. It also aligns with small-area spatial epidemiology’s emphasis that mapped outcomes often reflect joint contributions of environment and neighborhood context, and that spatial patterning itself is a substantive clue rather than a nuisance (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. In North Carolina, the broader premise that geographically referenced environmental conditions can be integrated with tract-scale social context to study population outcomes is well established (Root, 2019) [REF_5]; (Kalkbrenner, 2019) [REF_4]. Where this study extends those precedents is in using a statewide tract-scale linkage between hazardous-waste proximity and an asthma outcome, rather than focusing on a single metro area or a different health endpoint.

A study-specific qualification is that centroid-to-nearest-site distance is a construct-validity compromise: it likely introduces non-differential exposure misclassification (especially for large or irregular tracts), which would tend to attenuate associations toward zero rather than create spurious gradients. Thus, the presence of a detectable association in spite of this coarsening is consistent with (though does not prove) a spatially meaningful proximity signal.

#### 4.2.2 Why “contextual modification” appears weak in interactions but clearer in stratified contrasts
**Pattern.** In the full global interaction model, proximity-by-context interaction terms were not strongly distinguished from zero at conventional thresholds and the incremental explanatory power of adding the interaction set was modest. Yet stratified analyses indicated that the proximity association differed across median splits of poverty and Black population share, and the mean-difference comparison using a 3 km threshold suggested that proximity-related contrasts were concentrated in higher-poverty and lower-income tracts (with weaker or reversed contrasts in more advantaged terciles). This combination—weak global interaction evidence but stronger subgroup differentiation—is a notable feature of the results.

**Mechanism (with boundary conditions).** A parsimonious explanation is that effect modification was present but non-linear and/or geographically uneven in ways that a single global multiplicative interaction term could not capture well. Stratification can “surface” heterogeneity when the underlying relationship changes sign or magnitude across portions of the covariate distribution, while a global interaction estimates only an average departure from additivity-on-the-link-scale across all tracts. If contextual vulnerability amplifies the asthma burden associated with nearby hazards only after certain thresholds (e.g., high poverty environments where baseline housing quality, stress exposure, and healthcare access constraints are jointly worse), then tercile splits and within-3 km contrasts can show sharper differences than a global linear interaction. Conversely, if the modifying process depends on local political economy or metropolitan land-use regimes, then the modification is inherently spatial: a poverty–proximity interaction estimated statewide averages across very different regions, potentially diluting region-specific modification.

This mechanism would be weaker under boundary conditions where (i) social vulnerability is highly mobile or poorly captured by ACS tract measures, (ii) facility siting and neighborhood change are decoupled (e.g., recent gentrification near formerly industrial land), or (iii) the relevant vulnerability operates at a different spatial support (block groups, parcels, or school catchments) than census tracts, introducing MAUP-related smoothing that mutes modification patterns (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. In addition, stratified modeling can be sensitive to the cutpoints chosen (median vs tercile vs policy-relevant buffers), so apparent modification should be interpreted as descriptive evidence of heterogeneity rather than a definitive functional form.

**Engagement with prior literature.** The broader idea that environmental hazards and vulnerability are “entangled” and may be intersectional—so that the health relevance of proximity depends on who lives nearby—is consistent with environmental justice analyses emphasizing compound risks rather than single-axis exposure gradients (Cannon, 2021) [REF_7]; (Cannon, 2020) [REF_6]. It also resonates with applied GIS exposure research that treats proximity as a screening layer to be interpreted alongside neighborhood context, rather than in isolation (Shock, 2021) [REF_8]. A key nuance from this study is methodological: the evidence for heterogeneity depended on the analytic lens. This underscores a practical point for tract-based surveillance systems: if the objective is to identify vulnerable “near-hazard” communities for screening, stratified summaries and threshold contrasts may be more decision-relevant than reliance on a single global interaction p-value, particularly when the true heterogeneity is non-linear or spatially localized (Boulos, 2004) [REF_2]; (Kolak et al., 2019) [REF_11].

An unexpected element was that modification signals were clearer for poverty/income stratifications than for Hispanic composition, where coefficients were near zero in the global models. One plausible interpretation is construct-related: tract-level Hispanic share may not be a strong proxy for the specific pathways that link place to asthma prevalence in North Carolina at the tract scale, or the relationship may vary directionally across subregions such that the statewide average cancels out. This is a reminder that “demographic context” is not interchangeable across groups; compositional variables can represent different histories of settlement, labor markets, and urban form, which may or may not map onto facility siting and asthma vulnerability in a given state context (Sun, 2018) [REF_3].

#### 4.2.3 Persistent spatial autocorrelation and what it implies about omitted processes and model form
**Pattern.** Asthma prevalence showed strong global spatial autocorrelation, and meaningful spatial autocorrelation remained in the residuals of the global OLS model. This indicates that even after adjusting for proximity and tract covariates, nearby tracts tended to have similar asthma prevalence in ways the model did not explain.

**Mechanism (with boundary conditions).** Residual spatial autocorrelation is consistent with omitted spatially patterned determinants, spatial spillovers, and/or spatially varying relationships—core concerns in spatial epidemiology (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. Several plausible processes could contribute: unmeasured traffic-related air pollution corridors, regional pollen and humidity regimes, housing stock and indoor allergen environments, smoking prevalence, healthcare system geographies, and diagnostic/measurement practices that vary by provider networks. In addition, administrative health estimates can embed spatial smoothing or shared-data-generation processes that create correlated errors across neighboring tracts, especially in small-area health surveillance (Boulos, 2004) [REF_2]. A further possibility is that “nearest facility” does not approximate cumulative burden: if multiple facilities, industrial clusters, or other hazardous sources co-locate, then a single nearest-distance metric can leave spatially structured exposure residuals.

This mechanism would be less expected where the model includes the dominant spatial drivers at the appropriate scale (e.g., explicit PM$_{2.5}$ surfaces, traffic density, meteorology, and healthcare access measures) and where outcome measurement is uniform across space. Because this study intentionally used a limited set of tract-scale covariates, the remaining residual clustering is not surprising; it is informative, signaling where a global aspatial linear specification is likely incomplete rather than invalid.

**Engagement with prior literature.** Persistent clustering aligns with the foundational view that health outcomes exhibit spatial dependence due to shared contexts and diffusion-like processes (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. It also connects to applied tract-cluster mapping work showing that chronic disease outcomes often form stable tract clusters that invite localized etiologic inquiry (Kolak et al., 2019) [REF_11]. In asthma-related neighborhood research, attention to geographic identifier uncertainty and neighborhood assignment underscores how spatial structure can be both substantive and methodological (Bozigar et al., 2020) [REF_12]. In the present study, the residual Moran’s I suggests that future modeling should either incorporate explicitly spatial error/lag structures or move toward local models that allow relationships to vary regionally—especially if the goal is to support targeted environmental justice screening rather than statewide average inference.

A limitation that qualifies interpretation here is statistical-conclusion validity: OLS coefficients with robust SEs address heteroskedasticity but do not, by themselves, resolve spatial dependence in the error term. Spatially correlated residuals can make nominal standard errors too small and can overstate precision of associations if not modeled explicitly (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. Thus, the detected associations should be interpreted as evidence of patterned covariation rather than definitive estimates of uncertainty under a fully specified spatial stochastic process.

### 4.3 Closing the Loop with the Research Gap
The introduction identified a gap at the intersection of GIS-enabled health surveillance and environmental justice research: despite strong conceptual arguments and growing GIS capacity, routine tract-scale analyses that link environmental hazard proximity with health outcomes *and* explicitly evaluate context dependence remain limited, which can mask heterogeneity that matters for place-based screening and intervention (Boulos, 2004) [REF_2]; (Sun, 2018) [REF_3]. This study advanced that gap in two ways. First, it operationalized a statewide tract-scale linkage between hazardous-waste facility proximity and asthma prevalence—demonstrating that proximity signals coexist with, and are embedded within, strong socioeconomic and demographic patterning long emphasized in spatial epidemiology (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. Second, it showed that “effect modification” is method-dependent: global interaction terms suggested only limited average modification, while stratified summaries and threshold contrasts revealed sharper differences by poverty and income strata. What remains open—because the design is observational and uses simplified exposure metrics—is whether these patterns reflect facility-related exposures specifically, broader industrial and infrastructural co-location, or unmeasured regional drivers that are spatially correlated with both proximity and tract context.

### 4.4 Implications
For GIScience-oriented environmental health screening, the results imply that proximity-to-hazard metrics can contribute useful statewide signals but should be interpreted as part of a multivariate, spatially structured risk landscape rather than as stand-alone indicators. Practically, this suggests that screening workflows that map asthma burden alongside hazard proximity may be most informative when combined with tract vulnerability measures (poverty/income and racialized composition) and when analysts examine heterogeneity using stratified summaries that are easily communicable to public health stakeholders (e.g., near-versus-far contrasts within vulnerability strata), rather than relying solely on global interaction tests.

For environmental justice interpretation, the study’s juxtaposition of strong context associations with more modest interaction-model gains reinforces an important conceptual point: inequity can manifest as (i) differential *baseline* burden across social contexts, and/or (ii) differential *sensitivity* of burden to nearby hazards. The results were more definitive on the former—substantial tract context patterning—while the latter appeared in descriptive stratifications more than in statewide average interactions. This distinction matters for targeting: if baseline asthma burden is already higher in high-poverty tracts irrespective of facility proximity, interventions focused exclusively on near-facility buffers may miss large parts of the disparity geography; conversely, if near-facility contrasts concentrate in the highest-poverty and lowest-income strata, then multi-criteria targeting that requires both proximity and vulnerability may better align with environmental justice goals (Cannon, 2021) [REF_7]; (Cannon, 2020) [REF_6].

Finally, the remaining spatial autocorrelation in residuals implies that tract-scale surveillance systems should incorporate explicitly spatial modeling or localized diagnostics when the aim is inference about geographic patterning rather than only prediction. In line with the broader surveillance critique that GIS capacity does not automatically translate into decision-ready analytic routines, the results illustrate the need for operational toolchains that go beyond mapping—incorporating spatial diagnostics and locally attentive modeling as standard components of environmental health analytics (Boulos, 2004) [REF_2]; (Kolak et al., 2019) [REF_11].

### 4.5 Limitations and Validity
Several limitations qualify the interpretations and point to specific threats to validity. First, the exposure construct—centroid distance to the nearest hazardous-waste facility—inevitably mismeasured true exposure. This is primarily a **construct-validity** limitation: distance does not encode facility type, emissions, operational status, containment, meteorology, hydrologic connectivity, or cumulative burden from multiple nearby sites. Because this misclassification is likely non-differential with respect to tract-level asthma prevalence (i.e., not systematically more erroneous in high- vs low-asthma tracts), it would typically bias proximity associations toward zero, potentially underestimating the strength of any true exposure-related relationship. However, if facility registries are more complete in urbanized or better-resourced areas, then misclassification could be spatially patterned, complicating the direction of bias.

Second, the study relied on a cross-sectional ecological design, which limits **internal validity** and raises the possibility of residual confounding and ecological inference error. Unmeasured covariates that are spatially clustered—traffic emissions, housing conditions, smoking prevalence, or access to primary care—could plausibly explain part of the observed covariation between proximity and asthma. In such cases, the proximity coefficient may partially reflect correlated urban form or industrial land-use patterns rather than facility-related exposure per se. Relatedly, the associations are at the tract level and should not be interpreted as individual-level risk relationships.

Third, residual spatial autocorrelation in OLS residuals threatens **statistical-conclusion validity** by indicating that model errors were not independent. While HC1 robust standard errors address heteroskedasticity, they do not correct for spatial dependence; as a result, uncertainty may be understated, and some inferential claims could be overconfident. Addressing this would require spatial error/lag specifications, spatial filtering, or multilevel/spatial hierarchical approaches tailored to areal data (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1].

Fourth, the analyses inherit **MAUP and spatial support** limitations common to tract-based studies. Census tracts vary in size and internal heterogeneity, especially between urban and rural areas; centroid distances may be poor proxies in large tracts, and tract-level covariates can smooth within-tract disparities. These issues can attenuate associations and obscure localized modification, especially if vulnerability and exposure operate at finer geographic scales (Sargent et al., 1997) [REF_10]. The relatively modest incremental variance explained by interactions may partly reflect this smoothing: modification might occur within tracts or along micro-geographies (corridors, buffers) not captured by tract aggregates.

Finally, the study’s geographic focus on North Carolina constrains **external validity**. North Carolina’s settlement patterns, industrial geography, regulatory context, and demographic composition shape both facility siting and asthma vulnerability; the observed patterns may not generalize to states with different industrial histories or metropolitan structures (Root, 2019) [REF_5]; (Kalkbrenner, 2019) [REF_4]. What is supported despite this limitation is the methodological inference that, even within a single state, proximity–health associations can be spatially structured and context-embedded in ways that motivate local diagnostics rather than reliance on a single statewide summary.

### 4.6 Future Research
Future work can address these limitations and deepen the substantive and methodological interpretation in three concrete ways. First, to resolve exposure construct limitations, subsequent studies should replace nearest-distance proxies with richer exposure characterization—e.g., facility-type stratification, cumulative proximity/density metrics, and integration with modeled air toxics or PM$_{2.5}$ surfaces—so that “proximity” better approximates plausible asthma-relevant pathways (Sun, 2013) [REF_9]; (Shock, 2021) [REF_8]. Second, to address residual spatial dependence and potential spatially varying relationships, researchers should estimate explicitly spatial models (spatial error/lag, spatial Durbin) or local/hierarchical frameworks that can distinguish spatial clustering in outcomes from clustering in unobserved determinants, aligning with the core methodological agenda of spatial epidemiology (Elliott and Wartenberg, 2004) [REF_1]. Third, to improve causal interpretability and reduce ecological ambiguity, future designs could incorporate temporal structure (panel small-area asthma estimates, facility opening/closure timing) or quasi-experimental strategies (difference-in-differences around facility events, matched comparisons), complemented by attention to geographic identifier uncertainty in health data systems as highlighted in asthma disparities methodology (Bozigar et al., 2020) [REF_12]. Together, these directions would help determine whether the observed tract-scale patterns reflect facility-related exposures specifically, broader cumulative environmental burdens, or primarily social and infrastructural determinants that co-vary with hazardous land uses.
