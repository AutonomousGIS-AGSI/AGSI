## 1. Introduction

Depression is a major contributor to population morbidity, yet its prevalence is rarely distributed uniformly across space. In the United States, mental health outcomes exhibit pronounced geographic patterning that aligns with uneven distributions of material resources, opportunities, and exposures. A large body of work links neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES) to mental health through multiple contextual mechanisms—such as social interactions, perceived safety, and access to supportive environments—highlighting that “place” is not merely a backdrop but an active determinant of well-being (Jakobsen et al., 2022) [REF_2]. From a spatial science perspective, this framing motivates questions not only about whether SES gradients in depression exist, but also about what spatial processes may help produce or sustain them, including the everyday geographies of where people go, what environments they encounter, and how those activity spaces differ across socioeconomic contexts.

Prior research has established robust associations between socioeconomic disadvantage and depression risk across diverse populations and study designs. Meta-analytic and systematic-review evidence indicates that lower SES is generally associated with higher depression burden, although effect sizes and pathways vary across contexts and measurement approaches (Lorant, 2003) [REF_6]; (Jespersen et al., 2025) [REF_8]. Related epidemiologic work has long emphasized that neighborhood socioeconomic conditions are associated with health outcomes beyond individual characteristics, underscoring the need to consider contextual and compositional explanations jointly (Bosma et al., 2001) [REF_7]; (Lleras‐Muney et al., 2024) [REF_13]. Within GIScience and spatial epidemiology, this body of evidence has encouraged place-based analytics that connect areal health measures to neighborhood environments and resources, including applications using the CDC PLACES small-area estimates to examine spatially varying relationships between place characteristics and health indicators (Pellizzari et al., 2024) [REF_12]. Methodologically, a parallel literature has developed approaches to formalize causal pathways and decomposition of effects, including mediation frameworks that go beyond single-equation association models to quantify potential indirect pathways (Ochoa et al., 2023) [REF_3]. Together, these strands suggest a research opportunity: to examine whether mobility—conceptualized as observed patterns of place visitation—operates as one measurable mechanism that links structural socioeconomic conditions to spatial variation in depression prevalence.

However, important gaps remain in understanding whether and how mobility-derived measures mediate SES–mental health relationships, particularly in ways that are explicitly spatial, interpretable for policy, and scalable to common administrative units used in planning. Although Lee et al. analyze socioeconomic patterning in mobility reductions using mobility data, their focus is on short-term behavioral response during the early COVID-19 period rather than on mediation of socioeconomic gradients in mental health outcomes, leaving the role of routine place visitation as a pathway to depression burden underexplored (Lee et al., 2021) [REF_1]. Although Oyenubi and Kollamparambil use mediation logic to study depression scores, their mediator is socioeconomic status in the context of income loss (rather than mobility), and the empirical setting is South Africa, limiting inference about whether mobility-based mediators help explain area-level SES–depression gradients in a U.S. spatial epidemiologic context (Oyenubi and Kollamparambil, 2022) [REF_4]. Although Salelkar examines the relationship between depression and socioeconomic status, the study population is school-going adolescents and the design does not center spatially explicit mobility mechanisms, leaving uncertainties about whether mobility-derived exposures can be operationalized as mediators for population-level depression patterns in broader adult communities (Salelkar, 2021) [REF_5]. Collectively, these limitations point to an unresolved question: at an interpretable administrative scale (counties), do aggregate patterns of place visitation provide evidence of an indirect pathway linking socioeconomic conditions to depression prevalence, or do such mobility measures capture only a limited portion of the structural gradient observed in mental health?

This study addresses that question by examining South Carolina as a statewide case study where county health burdens, socioeconomic conditions, and built-environment opportunities vary markedly across space. The central research question is: *How do spatial patterns of mobility (place visitation) mediate the relationship between neighborhood socioeconomic status and depression prevalence across the counties of South Carolina?* To answer it, the study integrates county-level depression prevalence from the PLACES dataset with a standardized county SES index derived from American Community Survey indicators, and aggregated place visitation metrics computed from points-of-interest visitor data for all 46 South Carolina counties. The analysis pursues three linked objectives: (1) estimate the total association between county socioeconomic status and depression prevalence; (2) test whether mobility intensity—operationalized as overall visitation volume—mediates the SES–depression association; and (3) test whether mobility diversity—operationalized as the entropy of visitation distribution—mediates the SES–depression association. Ordinary least squares mediation models are used to estimate direct and indirect components, and residual spatial dependence is evaluated to assess whether remaining geographic structure suggests the need for explicitly spatial modeling.
