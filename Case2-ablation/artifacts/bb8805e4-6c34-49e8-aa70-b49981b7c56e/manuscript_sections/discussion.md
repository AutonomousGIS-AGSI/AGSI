## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal Findings
Across South Carolina counties, depression prevalence exhibited a clear socioeconomic gradient: counties with higher socioeconomic status (SES) had lower modeled depression prevalence, and this association remained robust to adjustment for the demographic covariates included in the county-level models. In contrast, the two mobility measures operationalized from aggregated place visitation—mobility intensity (overall visitation volume) and mobility diversity (entropy of visitation distribution)—did not provide evidence of an indirect pathway linking SES to depression prevalence in the formal mediation decomposition. Notably, SES was strongly aligned with mobility intensity (higher-SES counties had higher visitation volumes), but the visitation measures were not associated with depression prevalence once SES and covariates were accounted for. Residual diagnostics did not indicate remaining spatial autocorrelation in the fitted outcome models, suggesting that at the county scale and with the modeled covariate set, the principal structure in depression prevalence was captured without an explicit spatial error or lag term. The discussion below interprets (i) why the SES gradient appeared strong and persistent, (ii) why mobility intensity and diversity did not function as mediators in this setting, and (iii) what the spatial diagnostics imply for inference and future spatially explicit extensions.

### 4.2 Interpretation

#### 4.2.1 A persistent county SES gradient in depression prevalence
**Pattern.** The primary pattern was a consistent negative association between county SES and modeled depression prevalence across all outcome model specifications. The stability of the SES coefficient between the “total” model and the “direct+mediator” models indicates that introducing either mobility measure did not materially attenuate the SES–depression relationship.

**Mechanism (with boundary conditions).** This pattern is consistent with a broad set of contextual pathways through which socioeconomic conditions co-vary with mental health at the population level: differences in chronic stressors and material hardship, access to health-promoting resources, exposure to adverse built environments, and neighborhood social-interactive characteristics that shape support, cohesion, and perceived safety (Jakobsen et al., 2022) [REF_2]. At a county scale, these processes are plausibly aggregated expressions of multiple mechanisms operating simultaneously—some “compositional” (who lives in the county) and others “contextual” (what opportunities and stressors are prevalent). A key boundary condition is that county SES may be a weaker proxy for these mechanisms where within-county heterogeneity is high (e.g., counties containing both affluent suburbs and highly disadvantaged rural areas), because county averages can mask neighborhood-level deprivation and mental health burden. Similarly, the observed gradient may not generalize to contexts where depression prevalence estimates reflect different measurement regimes or healthcare access patterns, since diagnosis and reporting can vary with service availability.

**Engagement with prior literature.** The strong SES gradient aligns with longstanding evidence that lower SES is associated with higher depression burden (Lorant, 2003) [REF_6] and with more recent syntheses emphasizing that the relationship is robust across many settings while remaining heterogeneous in magnitude and pathways (Jespersen et al., 2025) [REF_8]. It also accords with work arguing that place-based socioeconomic conditions matter for health beyond individual characteristics, supporting the relevance of contextual explanations at areal scales (Bosma et al., 2001) [REF_7]; (Lleras‐Muney et al., 2024) [REF_13]. Within the PLACES-oriented spatial epidemiology literature, the finding is consistent with the broader value of small-area health estimates for identifying geographic structure in health outcomes and relating that structure to place characteristics (Pellizzari et al., 2024) [REF_12]. The present study extends that tradition by explicitly evaluating whether a plausible spatial process—routine place visitation—helps explain (i.e., statistically decomposes) that gradient, rather than only documenting covariation.

A study-specific qualifier is that the models adjusted for multiple race/ethnicity composition measures simultaneously, which induced substantial multicollinearity (as reflected in very high VIFs for percent White and percent Black). While robust standard errors were used, such collinearity can reduce precision and complicate the interpretation of the covariate block. Importantly, however, the central result here was not that these demographic covariates “explained away” the SES gradient; rather, the SES association persisted across specifications, which suggests the county SES index captured a depression-related structural dimension not absorbed by the included composition controls.

#### 4.2.2 Why mobility did not operate as a mediator at the county scale: measurement and competing processes
**Pattern.** The mediation decomposition did not support mobility intensity or mobility diversity as indirect pathways between SES and depression prevalence. Mobility intensity was positively associated with SES in the mediator model, yet it showed no association with depression prevalence in the outcome model conditional on SES. Mobility diversity (entropy) showed neither a strong relationship with SES nor an independent association with depression once SES and covariates were included. As a result, indirect effects were small and statistically indistinguishable from zero.

**Mechanism (with boundary conditions).** Several mechanisms could yield this combination of patterns—particularly the “strong $a$-path, null $b$-path” observed for mobility intensity:

1. **Mobility as opportunity rather than exposure (and cancellation of effects).** Higher visitation volumes in higher-SES counties likely reflect greater retail/service density, greater discretionary time, and travel enabled by resources—mechanisms similar to those invoked in studies of socioeconomic patterning in mobility behavior (Lee et al., 2021) [REF_1]. However, whether greater mobility corresponds to lower depression risk is theoretically ambiguous at aggregate scale: mobility may increase access to social interaction and services (potentially protective) while also reflecting longer commutes, time pressure, or exposure to stressful environments (potentially harmful). If protective and adverse components co-occur and vary across counties, net associations can cancel out, producing a weak county-level $b$-path even when SES strongly predicts mobility.

2. **Ecological mismatch between mediator and outcome.** Depression prevalence is a health state plausibly shaped by long-run exposures and cumulative stress, whereas visitation metrics derived from POI activity may capture shorter-run behavior, local economic activity, or tourism. When mediator and outcome reflect different temporal windows, mediation is not expected even if mobility is relevant at the individual level. This boundary condition is particularly important in ecological mediation: mediation logic assumes temporal ordering (SES → mobility → depression) in a way that cross-sectional county aggregates cannot verify (Ochoa et al., 2023) [REF_3].

3. **Scale dependency and the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP).** County-level aggregation can blunt the behavioral specificity needed to link activity spaces to mental health. “Mobility intensity” at county scale can conflate resident travel with visitor inflows, and it cannot distinguish whether mobility occurs in supportive versus stressful settings. If the relevant mechanism operates at finer spatial grains—neighborhoods, census tracts, or even individual activity spaces—then county means are an inappropriate mediator representation, and mediation would be weak or absent by construction. This is a classic boundary condition for areal mediation analyses: the mediator must be measured at the scale where the process operates.

**Engagement with prior literature (convergence and divergence).** The results complement rather than contradict mobility-focused work, because much of that literature documents socioeconomic differences in mobility (the $a$-path) without claiming that routine mobility mediates mental health gradients (Lee et al., 2021) [REF_1]. Where the current findings diverge from an intuitive expectation that “greater mobility may imply better well-being,” a specific reason is measurement: the mediator here was aggregate visitation, not individual-level mobility, social contact, or access to mental health care. Similarly, the mediation framework used is aligned with the broader aim of decomposing pathways (Ochoa et al., 2023) [REF_3], but the present study highlights a practical constraint: in observational ecological settings, plausible mediators can fail to register not because the underlying process is absent, but because the available proxy does not correspond closely to the theorized construct.

An additional unexpected nuance was that the “mobility diversity” model displayed relatively weak explanatory power in the mediator equation compared with the mobility intensity mediator equation. This suggests that entropy of visits—at least as operationalized from aggregated POI visitation—was not strongly structured by SES at the county scale in South Carolina. One plausible explanation is that counties may differ substantially in total activity volume while exhibiting broadly similar diversification across POI categories; in such a case, entropy becomes a low-variance measure that is poorly aligned with socioeconomic differentiation. This interpretation is conditioned on the entropy calculation and POI categorization: different taxonomies (e.g., finer-grained venue types) or restriction to resident-origin trips might yield stronger SES patterning.

#### 4.2.3 Spatial structure: limited residual autocorrelation after covariate adjustment
**Pattern.** Residual Moran’s $I$ diagnostics for the fitted outcome models did not indicate statistically detectable residual spatial autocorrelation. In other words, after accounting for SES (and the covariates and mediator), model residuals did not display strong remaining clustering.

**Mechanism (with boundary conditions).** One interpretation is that the county-level SES index captured a large portion of the broad regional patterning in depression prevalence—effectively absorbing the kind of spatially structured contextual variation that often produces residual clustering in spatial epidemiologic regressions. Under this mechanism, explicit spatial error correction may not substantially change point estimates at this scale because the major spatial gradients are represented by observed covariates. A boundary condition is that absence of detected residual autocorrelation is partly a function of statistical power and spatial resolution: with only 46 counties, Moran’s tests may fail to detect modest dependence, and county adjacency weights may be too coarse to capture functional connectivity (e.g., commuting regions) that structures mental health risk. Moreover, spatial dependence can be present in the data-generating process even if not evident in residual diagnostics, especially if the regression absorbs shared regional trends.

**Engagement with prior literature.** The finding resonates with the broader observation in place-health research that strong structural covariates (SES and related measures) can account for much of the apparent spatial clustering in health outcomes, even when those outcomes are visually heterogeneous in choropleths (Pellizzari et al., 2024) [REF_12]. At the same time, spatial modeling remains relevant not only for “fixing autocorrelation,” but also for interrogating spatial heterogeneity in relationships (e.g., whether SES gradients vary across regions). Methods such as geographically weighted approaches are designed for such heterogeneity, though they address a different question than the global mediation decomposition used here (Lessani and Li, 2024) [REF_10]. Thus, the spatial diagnostics here support the adequacy of a global county model for the narrow question of residual dependence, while leaving open the possibility that local relationships or multi-scale processes could be substantively important.

### 4.3 Closing the Loop with the Research Gap
The Introduction identified a specific gap: existing research has linked SES to depression and has documented socioeconomic patterning in mobility, but has rarely tested—at an interpretable administrative scale and in a U.S. spatial epidemiologic context—whether mobility-derived visitation measures constitute an indirect pathway connecting SES to area-level depression burden (Lee et al., 2021) [REF_1]; (Oyenubi and Kollamparambil, 2022) [REF_4]; (Salelkar, 2021) [REF_5]. This study addressed that gap by combining county-level PLACES depression prevalence with an ACS-derived county SES index and POI visitation-derived mobility metrics, then implementing a mediation decomposition alongside spatial residual diagnostics. The key advance is empirical and methodological: despite a strong SES–depression gradient and clear SES patterning in mobility intensity, the county-level visitation measures did not mediate that gradient in South Carolina. This result clarifies that “mobility” is not automatically a pathway variable when operationalized as aggregate POI visitation at county scale; rather, the mediator must match the theorized exposure mechanism (e.g., access to supportive environments, social interaction opportunities) in both construct and scale. The gap remains only partially closed: the analysis does not rule out mobility-mediated pathways at finer spatial grains or with individual-activity-space measures, nor does it establish causal mediation due to its observational ecological design.

### 4.4 Implications
Substantively, the findings reinforce the interpretation that structural socioeconomic context is a central correlate of geographic variation in depression prevalence at the county scale in South Carolina, consistent with place-based theories of mental health (Jakobsen et al., 2022) [REF_2] and broader evidence on poverty-health relationships (Lleras‐Muney et al., 2024) [REF_13]. Practically, the lack of mediation suggests that interventions or planning narratives that treat “increasing mobility” (in the aggregate visitation sense) as a lever for reducing depression burden are not supported by these county-level associations. Mobility intensity was higher in higher-SES counties, yet it did not correspond to lower depression prevalence once SES was accounted for; this pattern implies that visitation volume may be more reflective of economic activity, amenities, or population mixing than of protective psychosocial exposures relevant to depression.

Methodologically, the analysis illustrates both the promise and constraint of mediation frameworks applied to spatial public health data. Mediation decompositions are attractive because they force explicit articulation of pathways rather than treating covariates as a “black box” (Ochoa et al., 2023) [REF_3]. Here, however, the null mediation underscores that choosing mediators from passively collected mobility data requires careful construct validation: a mediator must be not only spatially patterned but also meaningfully connected to the health outcome at the relevant scale. Finally, the absence of residual spatial autocorrelation suggests that for this specific statewide county analysis, the primary gains may come from better mediator operationalization or finer-scale designs rather than from adding spatial error terms to the same county specification—though spatially varying coefficient approaches remain relevant for assessing heterogeneity (Lessani and Li, 2024) [REF_10].

### 4.5 Limitations and Validity
This study’s inferences are limited first by **ecological design and cross-sectional aggregation**, which threaten **internal validity** for pathway interpretation. Mediation logic assumes temporal ordering and (in causal interpretations) no unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding; neither assumption can be verified with contemporaneous county aggregates. If depression prevalence and mobility influence each other bidirectionally (e.g., poorer mental health reducing discretionary outings), the estimated $b$-paths could be biased toward zero or even change sign, obscuring any true pathway. Relatedly, the analysis cannot distinguish compositional from contextual mechanisms in a strong sense because individual-level SES and individual depression status are not modeled; this threatens **construct validity** for claims about individual processes based on area averages.

Second, the mobility mediators likely suffer from **construct mismatch and spatial misalignment**, primarily threatening **construct validity** and, indirectly, **statistical-conclusion validity**. Aggregated POI visitation at the county level may incorporate non-resident inflows (tourism, commuting) and may be driven by POI supply and reporting intensity rather than resident activity space exposures. Such measurement error in the mediator tends to attenuate mediator–outcome associations, biasing indirect effects toward zero and making mediation difficult to detect even if a true individual-level mechanism exists. The entropy-based “diversity” mediator is especially sensitive to categorization and the distributional properties of visits; if entropy varies little across counties or is dominated by ubiquitous categories, it becomes a weak proxy for “diverse activity spaces.”

Third, the inclusion of multiple race/ethnicity percentage variables simultaneously produced extremely high multicollinearity (as indicated by VIFs), which threatens **statistical-conclusion validity** by inflating standard errors and destabilizing coefficient estimates for the correlated covariates. While the primary conclusions focus on SES and the mediators (whose VIFs were modest by comparison), collinearity can still complicate adjustment because small changes in specification may alter how shared variance is partitioned among predictors. A more defensible approach in future work would replace mutually dependent composition percentages with a reduced set (e.g., one reference category omitted, or a smaller number of orthogonalized components), or explicitly model compositional structure via alternative parameterizations.

Fourth, generalizability is constrained by the **single-state setting (South Carolina) and county scale**, which threatens **external validity**. Counties differ widely in size and heterogeneity across the U.S.; in states with different urbanization patterns or regional economies, county-level mobility metrics may align differently with SES and mental health. Moreover, county-scale analyses inherently average over within-county inequalities that are central to neighborhood-health theory (Jakobsen et al., 2022) [REF_2], limiting transferability to neighborhood-level policy questions.

### 4.6 Future Research
Future work should prioritize mediator operationalizations that better match theorized mobility-linked mechanisms. First, to address construct mismatch and ecological bias, studies could use **origin-destination or resident-origin mobility data** (or device-based activity spaces) to compute mediators that reflect *resident* exposure to destinations, travel time burdens, or access to supportive environments, and then test mediation at **tract or neighborhood scales** where contextual mechanisms plausibly operate. Second, to resolve temporal ambiguity, a **longitudinal or repeated cross-sectional design** aligning mobility windows with subsequent depression estimates would strengthen inference about ordering and reduce bias from reverse association; this could be implemented by leveraging multi-year PLACES releases alongside time-indexed mobility aggregates. Third, given the plausibility of spatial heterogeneity even when residual autocorrelation is not detected, future analyses could examine whether the SES–depression association varies across the state using **spatially varying coefficient models** or related local-regression approaches (Lessani and Li, 2024) [REF_10], and then assess whether mobility mediators matter more in specific regional contexts (e.g., metropolitan counties versus rural counties), which would directly test the boundary conditions suggested by the present county-level null mediation.
