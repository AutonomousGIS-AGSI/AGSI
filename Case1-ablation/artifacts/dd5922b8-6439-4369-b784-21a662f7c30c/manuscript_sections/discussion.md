## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal Findings
This county-scale analysis of California in 2019 indicated that PM2.5 exposure was spatially heterogeneous and moderately unequal across the monitored subset of counties, with a clear metro–nonmetro contrast in average concentrations but little evidence that counties with higher income inequality (Gini) systematically exhibited higher (or lower) PM2.5. The bivariate PM2.5–Gini relationship was weak overall and remained weak after stratifying by urban–rural class, and the interaction model did not yield stable, interpretable evidence of effect modification given severe multicollinearity. At the same time, positive spatial autocorrelation in both PM2.5 and OLS residuals suggested that county observations were not independent and that key spatially structured processes were not captured by the linear specification. The discussion proceeds by interpreting (i) why PM2.5 exhibited strong geographic patterning and a metro–nonmetro mean difference, (ii) why income inequality did not align with county PM2.5 in this design, and (iii) what residual spatial dependence implies for model choice and inference.

### 4.2 Interpretation

#### Spatially clustered PM2.5 and a metro–nonmetro mean gap reflect regional atmospheric and emissions regimes more than within-county income structure
**Pattern.** County PM2.5 showed marked geographic clustering (positive global Moran’s $I$) and a higher central tendency in Metro counties than Nonmetro counties, even though within-group PM2.5 inequality (Gini across counties within each stratum) was similar in magnitude. Visually, higher PM2.5 concentrated in a subset of interior counties, while several coastal and northern counties were lower (Figure 1), consistent with a regionalized pattern rather than county-by-county idiosyncrasy.

**Mechanism.** A plausible mechanism is that county-average PM2.5 in California is shaped by spatially structured combinations of emissions density (transportation corridors, industrial activity, and residential combustion), meteorology (mixing heights, stagnation episodes), and physiography (basins and valleys that trap pollutants). These processes operate at regional scales that frequently cut across county boundaries, producing spatially autocorrelated exposure surfaces. Under this mechanism, “metro” functions less as a proxy for social structure and more as a proxy for emissions intensity and built-environment configurations that elevate population-weighted exposure, consistent with the rationale for administrative-area air pollution studies that often find strong place-to-place contrasts at county or city scales (Liao et al., 2019) [REF_8]; (Chen et al., 2022) [REF_11]. Boundary conditions are important: the metro–nonmetro difference would be expected to weaken (or reverse) in settings where rural economies are dominated by high-emitting point sources, intensive extraction, or frequent smoke episodes, or where metropolitan areas have aggressive controls and favorable dispersion. Similarly, at finer spatial scales (e.g., census tracts), within-metro variability can exceed metro–nonmetro differences because local proximity to sources and neighborhood segregation become dominant.

**Engagement with prior work.** The observed clustering aligns with the long-standing interpretation of Moran’s $I$ as evidence that values co-vary with those of nearby areas beyond chance expectations, reflecting spatial processes and interdependence (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. It also resonates with studies emphasizing that PM2.5 exposure patterns emerge from the interplay of emissions and atmospheric context rather than a single socioeconomic correlate (Li et al., 2025) [REF_12]. A study-specific qualifier is that PM2.5 was available for only 44 counties, and “county mean PM2.5” is a construct that may compress within-county hot spots and gradients; clustering at the county scale can therefore reflect both genuine regional atmospheric structure and the smoothing inherent in aggregation.

#### The near-zero PM2.5–income inequality association likely reflects construct mismatch and aggregation, not the absence of environmental injustice mechanisms
**Pattern.** Across the monitored counties, PM2.5 and county income inequality (Gini) were essentially uncorrelated, and the scatterplot showed wide dispersion in PM2.5 at similar Gini values (Figure 3). Stratification by urban–rural classification did not reveal a clear relationship in either subgroup.

**Mechanism.** One plausible explanation is **construct mismatch**: county-level income inequality summarizes the distribution of income *within* the county, whereas county-average PM2.5 summarizes ambient concentration *across* the county. Environmental injustice mechanisms—residential sorting, land-use decisions, and differential regulatory protection—often manifest as *within-region* disparities (e.g., near-road, near-port, and industrial-adjacent neighborhoods) rather than differences in county-wide means. In other words, a county can be highly unequal in income while having moderate average PM2.5 if high exposures are localized and offset by low-exposure areas, and conversely a county can have high PM2.5 due to regional transport or meteorology while displaying only moderate within-county income inequality. This mechanism is consistent with environmental justice syntheses emphasizing that hazards covary with disadvantage through spatial sorting and policy processes that operate at neighborhood and corridor scales (Banzhaf et al., 2019) [REF_14], and with calls to measure inequality explicitly and carefully match the inequality construct to the exposure construct (Machado et al., 2026) [REF_15]. The boundary condition is that if income inequality were tightly coupled to residential segregation across jurisdictional boundaries, and if high-income enclaves systematically externalized emissions to adjacent lower-income counties, then a county-level Gini could correlate with county-level PM2.5; such coupling is less likely when air pollution is dominated by regionally shared airsheds and transported secondary particles.

**Engagement with prior work and surprises.** The weak county-level association might appear surprising given broad environmental justice evidence of social patterning in pollution exposure (Banzhaf et al., 2019) [REF_14]. However, the apparent divergence is plausibly attributable to **scale and estimand**. Many environmental justice findings concern disparities by race/ethnicity or poverty status at sub-county scales or consider population-weighted exposure contrasts rather than county means. The present design instead tests whether a within-county income distribution metric (Gini) co-varies with a between-county exposure metric (county mean), which is a more stringent—and possibly ill-posed—link for capturing injustice mechanisms. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that measured disparities and inequality conclusions can be sensitive to analytic scale and domain choices (Li et al., 2022) [REF_16]. A further qualifier is statistical power and range restriction: the county Gini values varied within a relatively narrow band, and any true association—if it exists—could be too small to detect with the available sample size and the noise induced by monitoring-based county aggregation.

#### Urban–rural “effect modification” was difficult to estimate because the interaction specification was unstable, and spatial dependence persisted
**Pattern.** Adding urban–rural status and a Gini×urban–rural interaction improved in-sample fit relative to the bivariate model, yet individual slope terms were imprecisely estimated and variance inflation factors were extremely large for the urban–rural indicator and the interaction term. In parallel, Moran’s $I$ on OLS residuals remained positive and statistically distinguishable from zero, indicating spatial structure not captured by the regression.

**Mechanism.** Two linked mechanisms can account for this. First, **design imbalance and collinearity**: the monitored subset contained far fewer Nonmetro counties than Metro counties, and interaction models in such settings can become numerically unstable when the modifier is highly imbalanced and the predictor has limited independent variation within the smaller subgroup. Severe VIF inflation is consistent with an interaction term that is nearly redundant with its components under this sample structure, undermining interpretability of the conditional slopes. The boundary condition is that with a larger, more balanced sample—or with a continuous urbanicity measure rather than a binary metro/nonmetro classification—the interaction could be estimated with substantially less variance and clearer meaning.

Second, **spatially structured omitted variables**: residual spatial autocorrelation suggests that the model left out regionally patterned determinants of PM2.5, such as basin topography, meteorological regimes, wildfire smoke influence, or upwind emissions transport. This is not simply a technical nuisance; it implies that standard OLS uncertainty statements are optimistic because observations share spatial dependence (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. In air pollution settings, unmodeled meteorology and emissions mixtures are routinely emphasized as key drivers that can confound simple socioeconomic associations (Li et al., 2025) [REF_12]. The boundary condition is that if the analysis included appropriate spatial error/lag structures or region/airshed fixed effects (or otherwise captured key spatial processes), residual spatial autocorrelation would be expected to attenuate, and any remaining PM2.5–Gini association could be evaluated with more defensible uncertainty.

**Engagement with prior work.** The difficulty of interpreting interactions under spatial dependence aligns with broader spatial-analytic cautions that relationships can appear to change with model specification and spatial structure, and that ignoring spatial dependence can mislead inference about correlates (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. Moreover, environmental justice research increasingly frames pollution burden as cumulative and multidomain, suggesting that single socioeconomic indicators may be insufficient to capture contextual vulnerability at coarse scales (Kodros et al., 2024) [REF_7]. Here, the improved omnibus fit in the interaction model likely reflects the strong mean contrast between metro and nonmetro contexts rather than a robust, estimable difference in the PM2.5–Gini slope by urbanicity—a distinction that is important for interpretation.

### 4.3 Closing the Loop with the Research Gap
The introduction identified an underexplored question: whether county income inequality is meaningfully associated with county PM2.5 exposure in a statewide, policy-relevant administrative framework, and whether that relationship differs by urban–rural classification—an issue not resolved by work focused on other outcome domains (Bleaney and Nishiyama, 2004) [REF_1] or on scale sensitivity rather than the county-level PM2.5–income-inequality link (Li et al., 2022) [REF_16]. This study advances that gap by showing that, in California’s 2019 county cross-section, PM2.5 was spatially clustered and differed by metro status, yet it did not align in any clear way with county income inequality as measured by the Gini index, and attempts to estimate urban–rural heterogeneity in that association were hampered by instability and residual spatial dependence. These findings do not imply that socioeconomic inequality is irrelevant to pollution burdens; rather, they indicate that *county-level income distribution* is not a strong proxy for the processes producing *county-average* PM2.5 in this context, and that spatial structure must be addressed explicitly before drawing substantive conclusions about modifiers.

### 4.4 Implications
For GIScience and spatial environmental inequality research, the results underscore two practical implications about **measurement alignment** and **spatial specification**. First, the disconnect between county PM2.5 and county income inequality suggests that studies seeking to connect “inequality” constructs should carefully align (i) the scale at which inequality is defined (within-area distributions) and (ii) the scale at which exposure is summarized (between-area means). County-level Gini may be better suited for questions about intra-county socioeconomic structure (e.g., segregation, housing market polarization), whereas PM2.5 environmental injustice questions often require sub-county exposure surfaces or population-weighted metrics that can reflect near-source gradients. This implication directly follows from the observed dispersion of PM2.5 at similar Gini values and the weak correlations, which indicate limited explanatory value of county Gini for county mean PM2.5 in this setting.

Second, the persistence of spatial autocorrelation in residuals implies that statewide county regressions of air pollution should, at minimum, include a strategy for spatial dependence—whether via spatial error/lag models, region/airshed indicators, or spatially varying coefficient approaches—because otherwise inference about covariates can be distorted (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. Substantively, the results also suggest that urban–rural comparisons at the county scale may be more informative when framed as differences in baseline exposure regimes (as seen in the metro–nonmetro PM2.5 contrast) rather than as differences in the slope linking PM2.5 to within-county income inequality, unless sample balance and model stability are ensured.

### 4.5 Limitations and Validity
Several limitations qualify interpretation and point to specific validity threats. The most immediate is **incomplete PM2.5 coverage**: annual mean PM2.5 was available for 44 of 58 counties, implying nonrandom missingness is possible. If unmonitored counties systematically differed (e.g., more remote, sparsely populated, or with different emission profiles), estimates of statewide inequality and metro–nonmetro contrasts could be biased, threatening external validity. The likely direction is ambiguous: excluding very rural counties could understate statewide variability if those counties include both very low and episodically high PM2.5 environments (e.g., smoke-impacted regions), but it could also overstate variability if monitoring preferentially captures more polluted corridors.

A second limitation is **construct validity** related to exposure measurement. County-average PM2.5 aggregates heterogeneous within-county microenvironments and population distributions; it therefore cannot represent individual exposure inequality and may attenuate associations with socioeconomic structure (bias toward zero) when high-exposure communities constitute a small share of county area or population. This is particularly salient for environmental justice mechanisms that operate at neighborhood scales (Banzhaf et al., 2019) [REF_14]. Relatedly, the county Gini index captures within-county income dispersion but not the spatial patterning of disadvantage (e.g., segregation) that more directly links to exposure gradients; this mismatch likely biases any detectable county-level PM2.5–Gini relationship toward null.

Third, the regression results face **statistical-conclusion and internal validity threats** from (i) severe multicollinearity in the interaction model and (ii) residual spatial autocorrelation. Extremely large VIF values indicate unstable coefficient estimates for the urban–rural indicator and interaction term, inflating standard errors and making conditional-slope interpretations unreliable. Residual spatial autocorrelation indicates that OLS assumptions of independent errors were violated; this can yield underestimated uncertainty and overconfident inference about model terms even when coefficients appear nonsignificant, and it signals omitted spatially patterned covariates or misspecified functional form (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. In combination, these limitations mean the analysis is best interpreted as descriptive evidence about spatial clustering and about the limited *observed* co-variation between county PM2.5 and county Gini under a simple linear framework, rather than as a definitive test of whether income inequality relates to pollution burdens.

Finally, the design is **cross-sectional and ecological**, limiting internal validity for causal claims and raising the possibility of ecological fallacy. The analysis speaks to associations between county-level summaries in 2019; it cannot adjudicate temporal ordering, policy dynamics, or within-county exposure stratification. More generally, environmental inequality processes may unfold over longer time horizons and through cumulative exposures, which a single-year cross-section may not capture (Kodros et al., 2024) [REF_7].

### 4.6 Future Research
Future work should address three concrete needs raised by these results. First, to resolve construct mismatch, analyses should move to **finer spatial units** (e.g., census tracts or block groups) or use **population-weighted exposure surfaces** within counties, enabling tests of whether within-county inequality in exposure aligns with within-county income inequality or segregation measures; this directly targets the construct-validity limitations of county means and county Gini. Second, given residual spatial autocorrelation, subsequent studies should implement **spatial regression frameworks** (e.g., spatial error/lag models or spatially varying coefficients) or incorporate **airshed/meteorological region controls** to test whether the PM2.5–inequality association emerges after accounting for shared regional processes, building on the logic that spatial dependence is a substantive feature of pollution systems (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. Third, to improve inference about urban–rural heterogeneity and reduce instability, future designs should use **multi-year panels** to increase sample size and temporal leverage and should consider continuous urbanicity measures; panel settings also permit exploring whether income inequality co-varies with changes in PM2.5 through time, rather than relying on a single-year cross-section (Chen et al., 2022) [REF_11].
