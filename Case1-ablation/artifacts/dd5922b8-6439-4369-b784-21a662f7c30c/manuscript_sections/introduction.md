## 1. Introduction

Air pollution remains one of the most consequential environmental health risks worldwide, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a central concern because of its pervasive exposure pathways and well-documented links to adverse health outcomes. Beyond average exposure levels, the *distribution* of exposure across space and populations has become a core scientific and policy issue, as inequitable exposure is a defining feature of environmental injustice research. A large body of environmental justice scholarship shows that environmental hazards and associated risks are not randomly distributed, but instead tend to covary with social and economic disadvantage through mechanisms such as residential sorting, uneven regulatory protection, and land-use and transportation patterns (Banzhaf et al., 2019) [REF_14]. Recent syntheses further emphasize that PM2.5 exposure inequalities persist across many contexts and that measuring inequality explicitly—rather than relying on overall means alone—is crucial for identifying who bears the greatest environmental burdens and for designing effective interventions (Machado et al., 2026) [REF_15].

Methodologically, the study of exposure inequality increasingly combines inequality metrics with spatial analytical frameworks that can diagnose geographic clustering, spatial dependence, and heterogeneity in relationships across places. Inequality has been quantified using measures such as the Gini coefficient, coefficient of variation, and entropy-based indices (e.g., Theil’s $T$), which summarize how unevenly an exposure is distributed across observational units and can be compared across subgroups or contexts. In parallel, spatial statistics provide tools to characterize whether exposures are geographically patterned beyond what would be expected by chance; for example, Moran’s $I$ has a long-standing role in diagnosing spatial autocorrelation and can be interpreted as a regression-based measure of spatial dependence (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]. In air pollution applications, county- and city-level analyses have been used to model PM2.5 exposure patterns and associated health or risk outcomes using aggregated administrative units, in part because such units align with policy responsibilities and data availability (Liao et al., 2019) [REF_8]; (Chen et al., 2022) [REF_11]. Environmental justice studies also increasingly consider multidomain or cumulative risk frameworks that couple environmental exposures with socioeconomic stressors, underscoring the analytical value of integrating pollution metrics with measures of social inequality (Kodros et al., 2024) [REF_7]. Collectively, this prior work motivates integrated spatial-inequality analyses that quantify exposure disparities and then test how these disparities relate to socioeconomic conditions and contextual factors.

Despite this progress, specific uncertainties remain about how exposure inequality relates to *income inequality* and how such relationships may differ across urban–rural contexts, particularly when analyses are conducted at policy-relevant administrative scales. Although research has established that pollution burdens and socioeconomic conditions co-vary in many settings (Banzhaf et al., 2019) [REF_14], much of the inequality literature on the consequences and correlates of income inequality has been developed in other substantive domains (e.g., macroeconomic outcomes) and may not directly resolve how an area’s income distribution relates to its environmental exposure distribution. For example, although Bleaney and Nishiyama explicitly assessed whether inequality relationships differ across contexts, their focus was economic growth rather than environmental exposure, leaving open how income inequality relates to pollution burdens in place-based analyses (Bleaney and Nishiyama, 2004) [REF_1]. In addition, environmental justice estimates can be sensitive to spatial scale and zoning; although Li and colleagues demonstrated that environmental justice calculations for air pollution disparities change with modeling domain size and spatial resolution in Southern California, their work emphasizes sensitivity to analytical configuration rather than evaluating how county-level income inequality covaries with county-level PM2.5 in a statewide framework (Li et al., 2022) [REF_16]. Finally, while inequities in PM2.5 exposures have been examined for specific sources and time windows—such as long-term wildfire PM2.5 inequities in California—these studies do not necessarily test whether the association between PM2.5 and income inequality varies systematically by urban–rural classification at the county scale in a single-year cross-section (JA et al., 2023) [REF_17]. Together, these limitations leave underexplored whether county income inequality is meaningfully associated with spatial inequality in PM2.5 exposure, and whether any such association differs between metropolitan and nonmetropolitan contexts in California.

This study addresses these gaps by examining California counties in 2019 to evaluate how spatial inequalities in PM2.5 exposure vary with county income inequality (Gini), and whether the PM2.5–income inequality relationship differs by urban–rural classification. California provides a compelling setting because it contains substantial heterogeneity in settlement patterns and emissions sources—from dense metropolitan regions with complex transportation and industrial emissions profiles to less urbanized counties where different source mixtures and atmospheric processes may dominate—making it an informative case for assessing spatial patterning and contextual variation. The study integrates four county-referenced inputs: California county boundaries (58 counties), annual mean PM2.5 for 2019 (available for a monitored subset of counties), a county-level Gini index for 2019, and a county urban–rural classification distinguishing Metro versus Nonmetro counties. Building on established spatial and inequality analytics, the study operationalizes its aims through the following objectives: (1) quantify inequality in county-level PM2.5 exposure across California using multiple complementary inequality metrics (including the Gini coefficient, coefficient of variation, and Theil’s $T$), computed overall and stratified by urban–rural classification; and (2) evaluate the association between county PM2.5 and county income inequality (Gini), including whether urban–rural status modifies this relationship using a regression framework with an interaction term, alongside standard diagnostics for model stability and interpretability (Dray, 2011) [REF_6]; (Kodros et al., 2024) [REF_7]. Empirically, the study contributes county-scale evidence for California that connects exposure inequality measurement with a direct test of whether variation in PM2.5 aligns with variation in income inequality, and it does so while explicitly probing urban–rural differences that are often acknowledged but less frequently tested within a unified statewide design.
